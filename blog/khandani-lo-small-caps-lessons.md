# Chan’s reversal earns more on his S&P 600 file than on his S&P 500 file, and one year tells them apart only at the close

*Chan explains why the reversal he reproduces has a Sharpe ratio of 0.25 where Khandani and Lo report 4.47: most of their returns came from small and microcap stocks. He leaves the small-cap run as an exercise. On his own S&P 600 file over 2006, the rule’s Sharpe ratio is 3.4086 at the close against the S&P 500’s 0.2510, a gap more than three times its standard error. At the open the gap is +0.8850, inside one year’s noise. The result is exploratory, the file holds only survivors, and nothing in it says how many members were microcaps.*

## Why run the reversal on small caps

The [post on trading costs and failed companies](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md) reproduced a rule that Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo published (Khandani & Lo, 2007) and that Ernest Chan presents as Example 3.7 of *Quantitative Trading* (Chan, 2021). Each day the rule buys the stocks that fell most against the market and sells short the ones that rose most, so it profits when those moves reverse, which is why this post calls it the reversal. The stocks it owns and the stocks it has sold short together make up its **book**. On Chan’s file of S&P 500 prices over 2006, its Sharpe ratio, a measure of how well a strategy pays for its risk, is 0.2510 before costs, the figure behind Chan’s printed 0.25.

Khandani and Lo report 4.47 for the same rule in 2006, as Chan relays it (location 2099). Every location this post cites is a Kindle location in Chan’s book. Chan attributes the difference between 0.25 and 4.47 to the stocks tested. His backtest used the large companies of the S&P 500, and in their paper “most of the returns are generated by small and microcap stocks” (location 2137). At the end of Example 3.8 he suggests raising the Sharpe ratio by testing the strategy “on the S&P 400 mid-cap and S&P 600 small-cap universes” (location 2236), and leaves that as an exercise. Running that exercise would also test his explanation. Chan prints no figure for either universe, his word for the set of stocks a strategy trades.

No earlier run in [this post’s code repository](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading) had tested that explanation. Chan’s own code reads a file of S&P 600 prices, `IJR_20080114.mat`, for Example 7.4 later in the book. This repository ran the S&P 600 half of the exercise on that file, beside the S&P 500 file. Six lessons came out of it.

1. **At the close, the S&P 600 earns far more before costs, beyond one year’s noise.**
2. **At the open, the gap shrinks inside the noise**, because trading at the open lifts the S&P 500 far more than the S&P 600.
3. **Testing a gap between two Sharpe ratios** needs the correlation between the two books, and does not need their sizes matched.
4. **After costs of 5 basis points, or 0.05 percent, on each side of a trade, both universes lose money on the closes and keep a profit on the opens.** On the closes the S&P 600 loses only a little. The rule trades about as much in each universe at each price, so only the profit differs.
5. **A check for split prices flags two real moves**, and same-day news is what shows they were real.
6. **By the reading written down in advance, the S&P 600’s result points the way Chan says, and that is all it can say.** A later run on the S&P 400 finds the Sharpe ratio rising as the companies get smaller on the closes but not on the opens.

**Every result here is exploratory**, meaning it can justify a closer look but cannot settle a question on its own. The run applies a rule Chan published, unchanged, to the small-cap stocks his exercise names, for which he prints no figure. It can say which way the gap points on one year of survivors and how the gap compares with that year’s noise. It confirms nothing about Chan’s explanation. The code is open source at [l3a0/quantitative-trading](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading).

## The rule and the files

The rule is the one in Chan’s book, the code behind his printed 0.25. Each day every stock gets a weight equal to minus its return against the day’s average, divided by the number of stocks priced, and the weights are held for one day. Its Sharpe ratio is √252 times the mean daily profit over the standard deviation of daily profit, with no interest rate subtracted. A basis point is a hundredth of a percent. Each change in weight costs 5 basis points of the amount traded, which is Chan’s cost on each side of a trade, the buy and the sell alike. The [earlier post’s “How the examples work”](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#how-the-examples-work) builds each of these up.

The rule runs twice on each file, once on the closing prices and once on the opening prices. The second is Example 3.8, which updates the positions at the open, and the [earlier post’s Lesson 6](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-6-on-the-books-rule-trading-at-the-open-misses-chans-claim-after-costs) covers it. That lesson also describes another version of the rule, the one Chan’s Python notebook runs. This post leaves it out. When a stock’s prices are missing for several days, that version fills them with the last price seen, so the move across the whole stretch lands on a single day.

Vendors restate price history, so the same prices downloaded later can come back different. The data here is two saved files that keep the prices as they stood on the day each was saved, read over the same window of 251 trading days, 2006-01-03 to 2006-12-29.

1. **The S&P 600 file**, `IJR_20080114.mat`, saved 2008-01-15. It holds the 600 stocks of the S&P 600 as the index stood on 2008-01-14, with each stock’s price history running back from that date. On the window’s first day 592 of them have a price, and on its last day 594.
2. **The S&P 500 file**, `SPX_20071123.mat`, saved 2007-11-24, which the earlier post reads. It holds the index as it stood on 2007-11-23. No ticker symbol appears in both files.

Eight of the 600 stocks miss a price somewhere in 2006. Six have none all year, and two start trading during it. The rule gives no weight to a stock unless it has a price on both the day and the day before, so none of these missing prices reaches a position.

### What would point Chan’s way, and what would be noise

This repository wrote down how to read the run before computing any S&P 600 figure, in four parts. [Entry 44 of the replication log](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/docs/replication-log.md#entry-44-the-khandani-lo-reversal-on-the-sp-600-chans-quantitative-trading) records them beside every figure.

1. **What each figure is read against.** Each S&P 600 figure is read against the S&P 500’s at the same price and with the same costs.
2. **Direction.** If the S&P 600’s Sharpe ratio before costs is above the S&P 500’s at both prices, the result points the way Chan says. If it is above at one price only, the reading is mixed, and if it is above at neither, the result points against Chan’s explanation. The reading uses the figure before costs, because Chan’s explanation is about where the returns come from.
3. **Size.** Each gap before costs is read against its own standard error, which says how far apart two Sharpe ratios measured on one year could land by chance alone. Lesson 3 shows how it is computed. A gap more than twice its standard error reads as larger than one year’s noise.
4. **The run prints Khandani and Lo’s 4.47 and compares it with nothing.** It is a figure for 2006 too, but computed on the stocks they tested, which this repository does not hold.

## Lesson 1: at the close, the S&P 600 earns far more, beyond one year’s noise

On the closes, before costs, the two universes are far apart.

```math
\begin{array}{l|r}
\text{On the closes, before costs} & \text{Figure} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, Sharpe ratio} & 3.4086 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, Sharpe ratio} & 0.2510 \\
\text{Gap} & +3.1576 \\
\text{Correlation of daily profits} & 0.5258 \\
\text{Standard error of the gap} & 0.9872 \\
\text{Gap in standard errors, z} & +3.1987
\end{array}
```

This post rounds every figure it prints, and each figure computed from others comes from the unrounded values. So arithmetic on the printed cells can differ in the last digit or two.

A Sharpe ratio measured on 251 days is an estimate, and another year would give another number. The gap of +3.1576 is 3.1987 standard errors, the ratio the tables call z. That is past 2, the line written down before the run beyond which a gap reads as larger than one year’s noise. So one year of the closes can tell the S&P 600 from the S&P 500.

## Lesson 2: at the open, the gap shrinks inside the noise

On the opens the S&P 600 still earns more, though by less.

```math
\small\begin{array}{l|r|r}
\text{Before costs} & \text{Closes} & \text{Opens} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, Sharpe ratio} & 3.4086 & 5.3052 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, Sharpe ratio} & 0.2510 & 4.4202 \\
\text{Gap} & +3.1576 & +0.8850 \\
\text{Correlation} & 0.5258 & 0.3663 \\
\text{Standard error of the gap} & 0.9872 & 1.1640 \\
\text{Gap in standard errors, z} & +3.1987 & +0.7603
\end{array}
```

Trading at the open lifts both universes. The S&P 500 rises by 4.1693, from 0.2510 to 4.4202, and the S&P 600 by 1.8966, from 3.4086 to 5.3052. The S&P 500 gains far more, so most of what separated the two universes at the close is gone at the open. The gap of +0.8850 is 0.7603 standard errors, inside the line of 2, and one year of the opens cannot tell the two universes apart.

## Lesson 3: testing a gap between two Sharpe ratios

Two books traded on the same days earn on the same market moves, so their daily profits move together. A test of the gap between their Sharpe ratios has to account for that. Jobson and Korkie (1981) derived the variance of such a gap, and Memmel (2003) corrected it. Each book’s daily Sharpe ratio, s₁ or s₂, is its mean daily profit over the standard deviation of daily profit, without the √252. With those two ratios, the correlation ρ of the books’ daily profits and T days, the variance is:

```math
\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\hat s_1 - \hat s_2) &= \frac{1}{T}\Big[\, 2(1 - \rho) \\
&\qquad + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2 s_1 s_2 \rho^2\right) \Big]\end{aligned}
```

To get the standard error of the annual gap, multiply that variance by 252 and take the square root. A daily Sharpe ratio is small, so the first term in the brackets, 2(1 − ρ), carries 98 percent of the bracket on the closes and 94 percent on the opens. The correlation is therefore what sets the standard error. The higher the correlation, the more of each book’s noise the other shares, and the less of that noise lands in the gap.

On the closes, had the two books been unrelated, with ρ set to 0, the standard error would be 1.4252, and the gap of +3.1576 would sit 2.2155 standard errors out, barely past the line. The measured correlation of 0.5258 narrows the standard error to 0.9872.

The test needs no matching of the two books’ sizes. Chan’s weights are never scaled to a dollar amount, so the S&P 600 book and the S&P 500 book differ in total size. Multiplying a book by a constant scales its mean and its standard deviation together, so its Sharpe ratio does not change. Neither does the gap or its standard error.

The test rests on an assumption. The formula treats each day’s profit as independent of the day before and drawn from a normal distribution, and a reversal’s daily profits need not be either. So both standard errors are approximations, and nothing here measures how far off they are.

## Lesson 4: after 5 basis points a side, the closes lose and the opens keep a profit

Each Sharpe ratio after costs below comes two ways, which differ only on the first day. Chan’s code leaves the first day’s trades uncharged, and the earlier post’s [Lesson 2](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-2-matching-319-means-matching-how-chans-code-handles-one-missing-day) explains what that does. ### What an average day earns, pays and trades

An average day shows why some books lose after costs and others keep a profit. The [earlier post’s Lesson 1](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-1-on-the-sp-500-a-days-cost-is-larger-than-a-days-profit) reads an average day as a share of the position the rule holds. The two tables below do the same for all four books, one for each universe at each price, with the first day charged. Profit and cost are in basis points of the average gross position a day. Turnover is how many times the average gross position the rule trades in a day. The second table’s last column is the cost a side, in basis points, at which the average day’s profit and cost are equal, so the book breaks even.

```math
\small\begin{array}{l|r|r|r}
\text{Book} & \text{Profit} & \text{Cost} & \text{Turnover} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, closes} & 6.7197 & 7.3545 & 1.4709 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, opens} & 11.8569 & 7.3644 & 1.4729 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, opens} & 8.9815 & 7.3182 & 1.4636 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, closes} & 0.5276 & 7.2525 & 1.4505
\end{array}
```

```math
\small\begin{array}{l|r|r}
\text{Book} & \text{Cost over profit} & \text{Break-even} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, closes} & 1.0945 & 4.57 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, opens} & 0.6211 & 8.05 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, opens} & 0.8148 & 6.14 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, closes} & 13.7453 & 0.36
\end{array}
```

Every book trades between 1.4505 and 1.4729 times its average gross position a day, so every book pays between 7.2525 and 7.3644 basis points a day. The rule resets its weights each day from the previous day’s returns in every universe and at either price, so it replaces about the same share of its book. What changes from book to book is the profit. On the S&P 500’s closes the cost is 13.7453 times the profit. On the S&P 600’s closes it is 1.0945 times, which is why that book loses only a little after costs. Arithmetic on the printed cells misses the 13.7453 by the most, giving 13.7462, because dividing by a profit as small as 0.5276 magnifies that profit’s rounding.

### Whether 5 basis points is enough for small caps

Charging both universes the same 5 basis points flatters the S&P 600 against the S&P 500. Chan’s 5 basis points is his estimate for S&P 500 stocks (location 998). He also notes that low-priced stocks carry wider spreads between the price to buy and the price to sell (location 2639), and that small caps are often hard to borrow, so a short sale in a backtest may not have been possible (location 2730). The S&P 600’s closes break even at 4.57 basis points a side, below Chan’s estimate for the large caps. Its opens keep a profit up to 8.05 basis points a side.

## Lesson 5: a check for split prices flags two real moves

A stock that splits two for one halves its price overnight, and a file that misses the adjustment shows a 50 percent fall that no trade ever made. This repository has a check for that. It flags a day when a stock’s close moves by more than a factor of 1.6 from the day before, which means a close below 1/1.6, or 0.625, of the previous one, or above 1.6 times it. It does not know why a price moved, only how far.

On the S&P 600’s closes it flags two days in 2006.

1. **BCSI on 2006-02-06** closes at 12.37 against 20.165 the day before, a ratio of 0.6134. Blue Coat (BCSI) had warned that revenue and earnings would fall well below expectations, and the Motley Fool reported the warning that day (The Motley Fool, 2006).
2. **SCUR on 2006-07-12** closes at 4.99 against 8.07, a ratio of 0.6183. Secure Computing (SCUR) cut its quarterly revenue outlook after the close the day before, and TechPartner News reported a fall of nearly 40 percent in trading after the close (TechPartner News, 2006). No source states SCUR’s close on the day, so the match rests on the date, the size and the direction.

Both ratios fall just past the bound on the closes, and both stay inside it on the opens. This repository classified the two moves before computing any strategy figure, under a rule written down in advance: a real traded move stays in, and an unadjusted split or two companies joined under one symbol is set aside. Each is an earnings warning recorded at the prices that traded, so both stay in. The reversal bought each stock on its flagged day, and the next day’s return is in the run.

Chan writes that a drastic move “without any apparent news” is likely a passing liquidity event, such as a large holder suddenly selling, after which “mean reversion to the previous price levels is likely” (location 3109). These two moves came with news. So Chan’s own reasoning gives no particular reason to expect either stock the reversal bought on those days to bounce. This post did not measure whether either bounced.

This repository also has a function, built on the check, that refuses to run a strategy over any window in which the check flags a day. It would refuse this run on the closes because of these two days. Both days were checked against the news and kept, so the run does not call that function, and Entry 44 of the log records why.

## Lesson 6: the reading written down in advance points Chan’s way, and that is all it can say

Before costs, the S&P 600 earns more than the S&P 500 at both prices, which the reading set out at the start of this post counts as pointing the way Chan says. Four things limit what that result means.

1. **One year.** The run reads 2006 only, the year of Chan’s S&P 500 figures. The standard error says what one year can resolve, and on the opens one year cannot resolve the gap. [Another post](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/khandani-lo-reversal-lessons.md#lesson-3-most-of-the-rise-from-025-to-13-is-the-data-and-the-window) ran the version of this rule in Chan’s later book, *Algorithmic Trading*, on his file of 497 stocks from 2007 to 2011. It found that changing the data and the window moved the Sharpe ratio more than changing the rule did.
2. **Survivors.** The S&P 600 file holds the index as it stood on 2008-01-14. A small company that failed in 2006 or 2007 is missing, and a reversal buys exactly the stocks that fell. The [earlier post’s Lesson 5](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-5-the-toy-cannot-measure-survivorships-effect-on-the-reversal) explains why the missing stocks can push a long and short rule either way. Neither the size nor the sign of the effect is measured here.
3. **Nothing measured about microcaps.** Chan’s sentence names small and microcap stocks, microcaps being companies smaller still than small caps. The S&P 600 admits a company only above a minimum size, but the index does not drop a member just because the company later shrank, so some of its members may have been microcaps. The file holds prices and volumes but no market values, so this run cannot say how many. The result speaks for the S&P 600’s small caps against the S&P 500’s large caps, and says nothing about microcaps or the stocks outside the index.
4. **The run compares 4.47 with nothing**, as the fourth part of that reading set out. A figure here landing above or below theirs says nothing about where their returns came from.

Even so, the S&P 600’s result earns a registered test. That is a new run whose reading is written down before it starts, on data that neither this run nor the S&P 400 run described next has read.

### The S&P 400 keeps Chan’s predicted order on the closes and breaks it on the opens

Chan’s exercise also names the S&P 400, the mid-cap index, whose companies sit in size between those of the other two. His explanation predicts that the Sharpe ratio rises as the companies get smaller, from the S&P 500 through the S&P 400 to the S&P 600. A [later run in this repository](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/docs/replication-log.md#entry-50-the-khandani-lo-reversal-on-the-sp-400-chans-quantitative-trading) tests that prediction over the same 251 days. Like the S&P 600 run, it is exploratory and holds survivors only.

That run starts from the 401 holdings that the iShares S&P 400 fund, IJH, reported as of 2007-12-31, with prices from files downloaded in 2026 from the data vendor Alpha Vantage. A holding stays in the run only when it passes two checks.

1. Its price at the end of 2007 times the shares the fund held matches the value the fund reported.
2. Its prices start before 2006-12-28. The vendor starts many of its histories on that day whatever the company’s age, so a history that starts there is missing nearly all of 2006, even for a company that traded all year.

Of the 401, 285 pass both. Most of the 116 left out are dropped because of how the vendor serves their tickers today, and those 116 include companies bought or failed after 2007.

Before costs the S&P 400’s Sharpe ratio is 2.0338 on the closes, between the S&P 500’s 0.2510 and the S&P 600’s 3.4086. On the opens it is 3.4533, below both the S&P 500’s 4.4202 and the S&P 600’s 5.3052. So the predicted order holds on the closes and breaks on the opens, where the S&P 400 falls below the S&P 500. The reading written down before that run calls this mixed, since only an order that held at both prices would point Chan’s way. There are two steps at each price, from the S&P 500 to the S&P 400 and from the S&P 400 to the S&P 600, and each gets its own standard error, computed as Lesson 3 computes the S&P 600’s. Only one of the four steps is larger than one year’s noise, the closes’ step from the S&P 500 to the S&P 400, at 2.1973 standard errors. The step that breaks the order on the opens is about one standard error.

### Whether the change of source explains the S&P 400’s place

The S&P 400’s prices come from another vendor than Chan’s two files, downloaded in 2026 where his were saved in 2007 and 2008. So the S&P 400 run also rebuilds the S&P 600 from the iShares S&P 600 fund’s holdings on 2007-12-31, priced from Alpha Vantage like the S&P 400, and reads it against Chan’s S&P 600 file. The rebuilt S&P 600’s Sharpe ratio lands 0.1108 above that file’s on the closes and 0.7244 below it on the opens. Both differences are smaller than the S&P 400’s distance to its nearer neighbour at that price. On the closes that neighbour is the S&P 600, 1.3748 away, and on the opens it is the S&P 500, 0.9669 away. By the rule written down before the run, a change of source that small does not stop the run from reading the S&P 400’s place in the order.

That rule assumes the change of source moves the S&P 400 about as much as it moved the S&P 600, which the run cannot measure. Passing the rule also rests on three exclusions in the rebuilt S&P 600. This repository decided each one before computing any strategy figure.

1. A split the vendor had not adjusted, which sets aside one stock’s return on the split day.
2. A second unadjusted split, set aside the same way.
3. One bad price, which sets aside that stock’s returns on that day and the next.

With those three left in, both differences would be larger than those distances.

## What this replication cannot say

Five questions are beyond it.

1. **Anything about microcaps.** The S&P 600 file holds no market values, so nothing here says how many of its members were microcaps.
2. **What holding only survivors did to either universe.** Both files hold only survivors, the S&P 600 as of 2008-01-14 and the S&P 500 as of 2007-11-23.
3. **Whether the gap holds in another year.** The run reads 2006 alone.
4. **What a small-cap trader would keep after costs.** Both universes pay the same 5 basis points a side, while small caps cost more to trade, and nothing here measures how much more.
5. **Whether Khandani and Lo’s 4.47 came from small caps.** This repository holds none of the data behind their figure.

## What this means for a trader

One habit for each lesson.

1. **Put a standard error on a gap before reading it.** The gaps of +3.1576 and +0.8850 both point the same way, and only a standard error says that one year resolves the first and not the second.
2. **Run a comparison at every price the strategy could trade.** The close and the open told different stories about the same two universes.
3. **Use the correlation when two books trade the same days.** When their daily profits move together, as here, ignoring it overstates the noise in the gap. When they move against each other, ignoring it understates the noise.
4. **Find the cost a side at which a book breaks even, and compare it with what the stocks cost to trade.** When turnover is the same in every book, the profit decides which books survive costs, and the break-even cost says how much room the profit leaves.
5. **Treat a flagged price as a question to answer.** A bound on daily moves finds candidates, and dated outside evidence decides them.
6. **Write down before the run how to read its result, and say what the run cannot answer.** A result that points the right way on one year of survivors earns a registered test on new data.
