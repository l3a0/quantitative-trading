# Chan’s reversal earns more on his S&P 600 file than on his S&P 500 file, and one year tells them apart only at the close

*Chan explains why the reversal he reproduces has a Sharpe ratio of 0.25 where Khandani and Lo report 4.47: most of their returns came from small and microcap stocks. He leaves the small-cap run as an exercise. On his own S&P 600 file over 2006, the rule’s Sharpe ratio is 3.4086 at the close against the S&P 500’s 0.2510, a gap more than three times its standard error. At the open the gap is +0.8850, inside one year’s noise. The result is exploratory, the file holds only survivors, and nothing in it says which members were microcaps.*

## Why run the reversal on small caps

The [post on trading costs and failed companies](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md) reproduced a rule Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo published (Khandani & Lo, 2007), as Example 3.7 of Ernest Chan’s *Quantitative Trading* (Chan, 2021). Each day the rule buys the stocks that fell most against the market and sells short the ones that rose most. The stocks it owns and the stocks it has sold short together make up its **book**. On Chan’s file of S&P 500 prices over 2006, its Sharpe ratio, a measure of how well a strategy pays for its risk, is 0.2510 before costs, the figure behind Chan’s printed 0.25.

Khandani and Lo report 4.47 for the same rule in 2006 (location 2099). Chan puts the distance down to the stocks tested. His backtest used the large companies of the S&P 500, and in their paper “most of the returns are generated by small and microcap stocks” (location 2137). At the end of Example 3.8 he leaves the check as an exercise, testing the strategy “on the S&P 400 mid-cap and S&P 600 small-cap universes” (location 2236). Chan prints no figure for either.

Nothing in that explanation had been tested here. Chan’s own code reads a file of S&P 600 prices, `IJR_20080114.mat`, for Example 7.4 later in the book. This repository ran the S&P 600 half of the exercise on that file, beside the S&P 500 file. Six lessons came out of it.

1. **At the close, the S&P 600 earns far more before costs, beyond one year’s noise.**
2. **At the open, the gap shrinks inside the noise**, because trading at the open lifts the S&P 500 far more than the S&P 600.
3. **Testing a gap between two Sharpe ratios** needs the correlation between the two books, and does not need their sizes matched.
4. **After 5 basis points a side, the closes lose a little and the opens keep a profit**, because every book trades about as much as the others and only the profit differs.
5. **A check for split prices flags two real moves**, and same-day news is what shows they were real.
6. **The reading declared before the run points the way Chan says, and that is all it can say.**

**Every result here is exploratory.** The run varies a rule Chan published on data he left as an exercise. It can say which way the gap points on one year of survivors and how the gap compares with that year’s noise. It confirms nothing about Chan’s explanation. The code is open source at [l3a0/quantitative-trading](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading).

## The rule and the files

The rule is the one in Chan’s book, the code behind his printed 0.25. Each day every stock gets a weight equal to minus its return against the day’s average, divided by the number of stocks priced, and the weights are held for one day. Its Sharpe ratio is √252 times the mean daily profit over the standard deviation of daily profit, with no interest rate subtracted. Each change in weight costs 5 basis points of the amount traded, Chan’s cost on each side of a trade, where a basis point, written bp in the tables, is a hundredth of a percent. The [earlier post’s “How the examples work”](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#how-the-examples-work) builds each of these up.

The rule runs twice on each file, once on the closing prices and once on the opening prices. The second is Example 3.8, which updates the positions at the open, and the [earlier post’s Lesson 6](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-6-on-the-books-rule-trading-at-the-open-misses-chans-claim-after-costs) covers it. That lesson also describes a second version of the rule, the one Chan’s Python notebook runs. It is left out here, because it fills each gap in a stock’s prices with the last price seen and so reads a gap of any length as one day’s move.

The data is two **vintages**, meaning two saved files whose prices do not change.

1. **The S&P 600 file**, `IJR_20080114.mat`, saved 2008-01-15. It holds the 600 stocks of the S&P 600 as the index stood on 2008-01-14, with their prices carried backwards. On the window’s first day 592 of them have a price, and on its last day 594.
2. **The S&P 500 file**, `SPX_20071123.mat`, saved 2007-11-24, which the earlier post reads. It holds the index as it stood on 2007-11-23. The two files share no symbol.

The window is 2006-01-03 to 2006-12-29, 251 trading days, the same days in both files. Eight of the 600 stocks miss a price somewhere in 2006. Six have none all year, and two start trading during it. The rule gives no weight to a stock unless it has a price on both the day and the day before, so none of these gaps reaches a position.

This repository wrote down how to read the run before computing any S&P 600 figure, in three parts. [Entry 44 of the replication log](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/docs/replication-log.md#entry-44-the-khandani-lo-reversal-on-the-sp-600-chans-quantitative-trading) records them beside every figure.

1. **Direction.** If the S&P 600’s Sharpe ratio before costs is above the S&P 500’s at both prices, the result points the way Chan says. If it is above at one price only, the reading is mixed, and if it is above at neither, the result points against Chan’s explanation. The figure before costs is the one read, because Chan’s explanation is about where the returns come from.
2. **Size.** Each gap before costs is read against its own standard error, which Lesson 3 explains. A gap more than twice its standard error reads as larger than one year’s noise.
3. **Khandani and Lo’s 4.47 is printed and judged against nothing.** Their universe and window are not this file’s.

## Lesson 1: at the close, the S&P 600 earns far more, beyond one year’s noise

On the closes, before costs, the two universes are far apart.

```math
\begin{array}{l|r}
\text{On the closes, before costs} & \text{Figure} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, Sharpe ratio} & 3.4086 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, Sharpe ratio} & 0.2510 \\
\text{Gap} & +3.1576 \\
\text{Correlation of the two books' daily profits} & 0.5258 \\
\text{Standard error of the gap} & 0.9872 \\
\text{Gap in standard errors, z} & +3.1987
\end{array}
```

This post rounds every figure, and each gap, ratio and z comes from the unrounded figures, so arithmetic on the printed cells can differ in the last digit.

A Sharpe ratio measured on 251 days is an estimate, and another year would give another number. The standard error says how far apart two such estimates could land by chance alone. The gap of +3.1576 is 3.1987 of them, past the declared line of 2. So one year of the closes can tell the S&P 600 from the S&P 500.

## Lesson 2: at the open, the gap shrinks inside the noise

On the opens the S&P 600 still earns more, though by less.

```math
\begin{array}{l|r|r}
\text{Before costs} & \text{Closes} & \text{Opens} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, Sharpe ratio} & 3.4086 & 5.3052 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, Sharpe ratio} & 0.2510 & 4.4202 \\
\text{Gap} & +3.1576 & +0.8850 \\
\text{Correlation} & 0.5258 & 0.3663 \\
\text{Standard error of the gap} & 0.9872 & 1.1640 \\
\text{Gap in standard errors, z} & +3.1987 & +0.7603
\end{array}
```

Trading at the open lifts both universes. The S&P 500 rises by 4.1693, from 0.2510 to 4.4202, and the S&P 600 by 1.8966, from 3.4086 to 5.3052. The S&P 500 gains far more, so most of what separated the two universes at the close is gone at the open. The gap of +0.8850 is 0.7603 standard errors, inside the line of 2, and one year of the opens cannot tell the two universes apart.

*Each line sums each day’s profit through 2006, as a share of that book’s average gross position, the size of its long and short positions added together. Chan’s code leaves the first day’s trades uncharged and that day’s profit after costs missing, and a running total cannot carry a missing value. So the dashed lines charge the first day, and they show Lesson 4’s charged figures, −0.3255 and 2.0257 for the S&P 600, rather than Chan’s uncharged −0.3702 and 2.0147. This repository measured the running totals after the run, and declared the test behind each heading before computing any figure. On the opens the solid lines end further apart than a z of +0.7603 suggests, because the distance between two running totals says nothing about how much the daily profits swing.*

## Lesson 3: testing a gap between two Sharpe ratios

Two books traded on the same days earn on the same market moves, so their daily profits move together. A test of the gap between their Sharpe ratios has to account for that. Jobson and Korkie (1981) derived the variance of such a gap, and Memmel (2003) corrected it. With each book’s daily Sharpe ratio s₁ and s₂, the correlation ρ of their daily profits and T days, the variance is:

```math
\operatorname{Var}(\hat s_1 - \hat s_2) = \frac{1}{T}\left[2(1 - \rho) + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2 s_1 s_2 \rho^2\right)\right]
```

The standard error of the annual gap is the square root of 252 times that variance. A daily Sharpe ratio is small, so the first term in the brackets, 2(1 − ρ), carries 98 percent of the bracket on the closes and 94 percent on the opens. The correlation is therefore what sets the standard error. The higher it is, the more of each book’s noise the other shares, and the less of it lands in the gap.

On the closes, had the two books been unrelated, with ρ set to 0, the standard error would be 1.4252, and the gap of +3.1576 would sit 2.2155 standard errors out, barely past the line. The measured correlation of 0.5258 narrows the standard error to 0.9872.

The test needs no matching of the two books’ sizes. Chan’s weights are never scaled to a dollar amount, so the S&P 600 book and the S&P 500 book hold different positions. Multiplying a book by a constant scales its mean and its standard deviation together, so its Sharpe ratio does not change. Neither does the gap or its standard error.

The price is an assumption. The formula treats each day’s profit as independent of the day before and drawn from a normal distribution, and a reversal’s daily profits are neither. So both standard errors are approximations, and nothing here measures how far off they are.

## Lesson 4: after 5 basis points a side, the closes lose and the opens keep a profit

The after-cost figures come in two specifications, so each figure below names its own. Chan’s code leaves the first day’s trades uncharged, and the earlier post’s [Lesson 2](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-2-matching-319-means-matching-how-chans-code-handles-one-missing-day) explains what that does. Charging them gives the second row of each pair.

```math
\begin{array}{l|r|r|r}
\text{After 5 bp a side} & \text{S}\&\text{P 600} & \text{S}\&\text{P 500} & \text{Gap} \\ \hline
\text{Closes, first day uncharged, as Chan's code} & -0.3702 & -3.1884 & +2.8181 \\
\text{Closes, first day charged} & -0.3255 & -3.2337 & +2.9081 \\
\text{Opens, first day uncharged, as Chan's code} & 2.0147 & 0.7834 & +1.2313 \\
\text{Opens, first day charged} & 2.0257 & 0.8293 & +1.1964
\end{array}
```

Every gap after costs is positive. On the closes the S&P 600 still loses money after costs, though far less than the S&P 500. On the opens both keep a profit.

The average day says why. The [earlier post’s Lesson 1](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-1-on-the-sp-500-a-days-cost-is-larger-than-a-days-profit) reads an average day as a share of the position the rule holds, and the table below does the same for all four books, on the charged specification. Profit and cost are in basis points of the average gross position a day. Turnover is how many times the average gross position the rule trades in a day. The last column is the cost a side at which the average day’s profit and cost are equal, so the book breaks even.

```math
\begin{array}{l|r|r|r|r|r}
\text{Book} & \text{Profit} & \text{Cost} & \text{Turnover} & \text{Cost over profit} & \text{Break-even, bp a side} \\ \hline
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, closes} & 6.7197 & 7.3545 & 1.4709 & 1.0945 & 4.57 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 600, opens} & 11.8569 & 7.3644 & 1.4729 & 0.6211 & 8.05 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, opens} & 8.9815 & 7.3182 & 1.4636 & 0.8148 & 6.14 \\
\text{S}\&\text{P 500, closes} & 0.5276 & 7.2525 & 1.4505 & 13.7453 & 0.36
\end{array}
```

Every book trades between 1.4505 and 1.4729 times its average gross position a day, so every book pays between 7.2525 and 7.3644 basis points a day. The rule resets its weights each day from the day before’s returns in every universe and at either price, so it replaces about the same share of its book. What changes from book to book is the profit. On the S&P 500’s closes the cost is 13.7453 times the profit. On the S&P 600’s closes it is 1.0945 times, which is why that book loses only a little after costs.

Charging both universes the same 5 basis points flatters the S&P 600 against the S&P 500. Chan’s 5 basis points is his estimate for S&P 500 stocks (location 998). He also notes that low-priced stocks carry wider spreads between the price to buy and the price to sell (location 2639), and that small caps are often hard to borrow, so a short sale in a backtest may not have been possible (location 2730). The S&P 600’s closes break even at 4.57 basis points a side, below Chan’s estimate for the large caps. Its opens keep a profit up to 8.05 basis points a side.

## Lesson 5: a check for split prices flags two real moves

A stock that splits two for one halves its price overnight, and a file that misses the adjustment shows a fall of half that never traded. This repository has a check for that. It flags a day when a stock’s close moves by more than a factor of 1.6 from the day before, which means a close below 1/1.6, or 0.625, of the previous one, or above 1.6 times it. It does not know why a price moved, only how far.

On the S&P 600’s closes it flags two days in 2006.

1. **BCSI on 2006-02-06** closes at 12.37 against 20.165 the day before, a ratio of 0.6134. Blue Coat had warned that revenue and earnings would fall well below expectations, and the Motley Fool reported the warning that day (The Motley Fool, 2006).
2. **SCUR on 2006-07-12** closes at 4.99 against 8.07, a ratio of 0.6183. Secure Computing cut its quarterly revenue outlook after the close the day before, and TechPartner News reported a fall of nearly 40 percent in trading after the close (TechPartner News, 2006). No source states SCUR’s close on the day, so the match rests on the date, the size and the direction.

Both ratios fall just past the bound on the closes, and both stay inside it on the opens. This repository classified the two moves before computing any strategy figure, under a rule written down in advance: a real traded move stays in, and an unadjusted split or two companies joined under one symbol is set aside. Each is an earnings warning recorded at the prices that traded, so both stay in. The reversal bought each stock on its flagged day, and the next day’s return is in the run. This repository also has a guard built on the check, which refuses to run a strategy over a window in which the check flags any day. On the closes it would refuse the run over these two days, so the run does not call it, and Entry 44 of the log records why.

Chan writes that a drastic move “without any apparent news” is likely a passing liquidity event, after which “mean reversion to the previous price levels is likely” (location 3109). These two moves came with news. So the reversal bought both on days Chan’s own reasoning gives no particular cause to expect a bounce. Whether either bounced is not something this post measured.

## Lesson 6: the declared reading points Chan’s way, and that is all it can say

Before costs, the S&P 600 earns more than the S&P 500 at both prices. Under the reading declared before the run, that points the way Chan says. Four things limit what it means.

1. **One year.** The run reads 2006 only, the year of Chan’s S&P 500 figures. The standard error says what one year can resolve, and on the opens one year cannot resolve the gap. The [post on Chan’s 2012 panel](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/khandani-lo-reversal-lessons.md#lesson-3-most-of-the-rise-from-025-to-13-is-the-data-and-the-window) found that a change of data and window moved a close cousin of this rule more than the change of rule did.
2. **Survivors.** The S&P 600 file holds the index as it stood on 2008-01-14. A small company that failed in 2006 or 2007 is missing, and a reversal buys exactly the stocks that fell. The [earlier post’s Lesson 5](https://github.com/l3a0/quantitative-trading/blob/main/blog/survivorship-and-transaction-costs.md#lesson-5-the-toy-cannot-measure-survivorships-effect-on-the-reversal) explains why the missing stocks can push a long and short rule either way. Neither the size nor the sign of the effect is measured here.
3. **Nothing measured about microcaps.** Chan’s sentence names small and microcap stocks. The S&P 600 admits a company only above a minimum size, but it does not drop a member only because the member later shrank, so some of its members may have been microcaps. The file holds prices and volumes without market values, so this run cannot say how many. The result speaks for the S&P 600’s small caps against the S&P 500’s large caps, and for nothing about microcaps.
4. **4.47 is judged against nothing**, as the declared reading set out. A figure here landing above or below theirs says nothing about where their returns came from.

Chan’s exercise also names the S&P 400, the mid-cap index. It is the third point, between the other two, and it has not been run here. A reading that points Chan’s way earns a test registered in advance, on data this run has not seen.

## What this replication cannot say

Five questions are beyond it.

1. **Anything about microcaps.** The file holds no market values, so nothing here says which S&P 600 members were microcaps.
2. **What survivorship did to either universe.** Both files hold only survivors, the S&P 600 as of 2008-01-14 and the S&P 500 as of 2007-11-23.
3. **Whether the gap holds in another year.** The run reads 2006 alone.
4. **What a small-cap trader would keep after costs.** Both universes pay the same 5 basis points a side, while small caps cost more to trade, and nothing here prices by how much.
5. **Whether Khandani and Lo’s 4.47 came from small caps.** This repository holds none of the data behind their figure.

## What this means for a trader

One habit for each lesson.

1. **Put a standard error on a gap before reading it.** The gaps of +3.1576 and +0.8850 both point the same way, and only a standard error says that one year resolves the first and not the second.
2. **Run a comparison at every price the strategy could trade.** The close and the open told different stories about the same two universes.
3. **Use the correlation when two books trade the same days.** When their daily profits move together, as here, ignoring it overstates the noise in the gap. When they move against each other, ignoring it understates the noise.
4. **Find the cost a side at which a book breaks even, and compare it with what the stocks cost to trade.** When turnover is the same in every book, the profit decides, and the break-even cost says how much room the profit leaves.
5. **Treat a flagged price as a question to answer.** A bound on daily moves finds candidates, and dated outside evidence decides them.
6. **Write the reading down before the run, and say what it cannot reach.** A result that points the right way on one year of survivors earns a registered test on new data.
